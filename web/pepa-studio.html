Welcome

This tutorial starts from school maths and builds up step by step. Each step has a picture, and many have a button that loads an example into the tool so you can see it for yourself.

The Studio answers one question in many forms: do two systems behave the same? A system can be a garden bed, a herd, a farm plan or a machine. “Behave the same” sounds simple, but it gets interesting when the behaviour never ends (a garden is harvested every week, year after year) and when things happen by chance and at different speeds.

  1. Steps 2–7 explain the ideas with pictures: states and arrows, comparing behaviour, induction and coinduction, and finding a difference.
  2. Steps 8–13 add chance and time, and show what each tab of the Studio does.
  3. Steps 14–15 are the precise mathematics, for readers who want it.
  4. Step 16 has exercises with answers; step 17 lists the sources.

Systems are states and arrows

Describe a system by the situations it can be in (states) and the events that move it from one to the next (arrows, labelled with the event). A tomato plant grows, flowers, fruits, is picked again and again, and rests over winter:

The arrow from Fruiting back to itself is the harvest: it can happen again and again. The long arrow back to Growing makes the whole thing a cycle. Nothing here ever “ends”, and that is exactly the kind of behaviour this tool is built for.

In the Studio such systems are written in a small language called PEPA (step 10). The State space tab draws the states and arrows of whatever model is in the editor.

When do two systems behave the same?

Two clocks: A has two states, B has four. Both go tick, tock, tick, tock, for ever. Are they “the same”? Counting states says no. Watching them says yes: whatever one does, the other can do too.

The dashed lines are a claim: A0 behaves like B0 and B2, and A1 behaves like B1 and B3. Check the claim one step at a time. From a paired couple, whatever step one side takes, the other side can take the same step, and the two new states are paired again. It never fails, so it will never fail however long the clocks run. A claim that passes this check is called a bisimulation, and the two systems are bisimilar.

You can see the check as a game between a challenger, who tries to show a difference, and a defender, who answers every move with the same move on the other side. The systems are bisimilar exactly when the defender can keep answering for ever.

Induction: proving things that are built up step by step

The natural numbers are built from 0 by “add one”, again and again. To prove that something holds for every number, induction needs two things:

  1. Base case: it holds for 0.
  2. Step: if it holds for n, it holds for n + 1.

Like a row of dominoes: the first falls (base case), and each one knocks over the next (step), so all of them fall. It works because every number is reached from 0 in finitely many steps.

Claim: 1 + 2 + … + n = n(n + 1) / 2 Base case (n = 1): 1 = 1·2/2 ✓ Step: if it holds for n, add n + 1 to both sides: n(n + 1)/2 + (n + 1) = (n + 1)(n + 2)/2 ✓

Induction suits things that are finished: a number, a list, a season’s harvest record. In this tool it appears whenever the answer is “these two systems differ”: a difference always shows up after finitely many steps, so it can be written down as a finite piece of evidence (step 7).

Coinduction: proving things about behaviour that never ends

Induction builds numbers upward with “add one”, starting from 0. Coinduction runs the other way: it takes things apart with “take one away”, asking only “is there anything left, and if so, what remains after one step?”. Taking one away from 3 gives 2, then 1, then 0, and the unfolding stops. But it also allows a value that never runs out: ∞, for which taking one away gives ∞ again. Numbers seen this way, including ∞, are the conatural numbers.

Never-ending behaviour is like ∞: a clock that ticks for ever, the decimal 0.333…, a garden harvested every week. There is no last step and no base case to start from. So coinduction does not build up from a beginning. It starts from a claim (“these two behave the same”) and checks that one step later you are back inside the claim (step 3). If that holds, the claim holds for ever.

The same mirror shows up in how the two kinds of proof find their answer. In the illustration below, induction starts with nothing proved and adds facts until nothing new follows. Coinduction starts by believing every pairing and removes the ones that are caught out, until the rest survives. Both stop at a fixed point: induction at the smallest one, coinduction at the largest one.

The Studio’s comparison works exactly like the falling line: it begins by treating all states as equal and splits groups only when one can do something the other cannot (step 14 has the details).

Depth first and breadth first: a picture of the two

Every system unfolds into a tree of possible runs: the root is the start, and each branch is a step it can take. Two ways to walk a tree give a helpful picture of induction and coinduction. It is a picture, not a theorem: proofs can be organised in many ways. What matters is that induction needs the bottom of the tree and coinduction does not.

Pick a walk to see the order in which the tree is visited.

Depth first, finishing the children before the parent is how induction proves a fact about a finished tree. It goes down a branch to the leaves (the base cases), proves them, and climbs back up: a node is done once everything below it is done. This needs leaves at the bottom.

Breadth first, one level at a time fits never-ending behaviour, where the tree has no bottom (the dotted lines). After checking level k you know that the two systems agree for their first k steps. The Studio’s comparison grows in exactly this way, round by round. If the systems differ, the difference appears at some finite level, so the walk finds it. If they never differ, every level agrees, and the claim from step 3 settles all levels at once.

When they differ: a formula as evidence

When two systems are not the same, the Studio does not just say “no”. It gives evidence: a short formula that is true for one system and false for the other. Read it left to right:

⟨disease⟩⟨healing⟩tt “can get the disease, and then can heal” ¬⟨b⟩tt “cannot do b right now” ⟨start⟩=2 tt “starts at a total rate of 2”

⟨a⟩ means “can do a, and then…”, tt means “true”, and ¬ means “not”. Because a difference always shows up after finitely many steps (step 4), there is always such a formula, and you can check it by hand against the two models.

On the Compare tab, click a verdict to highlight the states where its formula holds.

Adding chance and time: rates

Real systems do not just “can” or “cannot”: things happen at some speed, by chance. Each arrow gets a rate: how often it happens per unit of time. A rate of 0.1 per day means about once every 10 days on average (1 / 0.1).

When several things can happen, they race: whichever happens first wins. The chance of each is its share of the total rate, and the time until something happens is, on average, one over the total. With rates, “the same behaviour” also means “at the same speeds”, and the Studio checks that too (Markovian bisimulation).

The Over time tab shows the expected number of animals in each state as time passes, computed from these rates.

Merging states that behave alike: lumping

Three animals, one of them sick. Whether it is the first, second or third animal does not change what can happen next: all three situations behave the same. So they can be merged into one state, “one sick animal”, without losing anything you can observe.

This is called lumping, and it is what the comparison of step 3 is for in practice: it finds every group of states that behave the same and merges them. A model with many identical animals or plants can shrink enormously, and every answer about the lumped model is exactly the same as for the original.

On the State space tab, switch between Full and Lumped. In the full graph, states with the same colour merge.

Building big systems from small parts

Writing down every state of a herd by hand is hopeless. Instead, describe each part on its own and say how the parts work together. The Studio uses PEPA for this:

Healthy = (disease, 0.02).Sick; an animal's own life Sick = (healing, 0.1).Healthy; Vet = (healing, infty).Vet; the vet takes part in every healing Healthy[3] <healing> Vet three animals, working with the vet on "healing"
  • (a, r).P: do a at rate r, then behave as P.
  • P + Q: whichever happens first.
  • P <a> Q: P and Q run side by side and must do a together; P || Q: side by side, independently.
  • P[3]: three copies.

The Studio works out all the states and arrows from this description. The Components tab shows each part on its own, and the State space tab shows the whole. Comparing parts is safe: if two parts behave the same, then swapping one for the other in a bigger system does not change how the whole behaves. This is proved in Lean in this project.

Comparing while it runs: continuous harvest (§11)

The motivation. Classical agriculture judges a crop or a herd by its yield at harvest or culling, so during growth the system is effectively unmonitored: a deviation shows up only when it can no longer be corrected. In permaculture the harvest is continuous — fruit, leaves and eggs keep coming, and the system may never “end” — so there is no terminal yield to wait for. The comparison with a plan or a benchmark has to be made while the system is running, on behaviour rather than on a final number.

Why bisimulation fits. Bisimulation compares what a system can do, step by step, and it is coinductive (§1): a cyclic or never-ending behaviour is compared without being unrolled to an end. That matches a food forest or a laying flock, whose behaviour is a cycle (flower, fruit, harvest, regrow) and not a path to one outcome. Three things follow.

1. Structure: does the real system have the same possible behaviours as the plan (the stages it reaches, whether the cycle closes, whether it regrows)? 2. Explanation: when it does not, a distinguishing formula (§4) says why, e.g. "the plan can fruit and then regrow; this plot never regrows". 3. Rates: Markovian bisimulation (§7) also requires the rates to match, and the long-run throughput (harvests per week at equilibrium) is the natural one-number summary for a system that does not end. Bisimilar systems have equal throughput.

Verdicts on a running prefix. Only part of the behaviour has been observed so far, and a transition that is missing now may simply not have happened yet. A sound verdict on a prefix therefore reports a difference only when it can no longer be repaired: an observed move has no matching answer from a state declared complete. Until then the answer is “holds so far”. The PrefixChecker in bisim-refine implements exactly this, updating its answer in place as each event arrives; the Stream tab replays three harvest seasons through it, one event at a time.

Where exact bisimulation breaks. Real rates are never exactly equal (§10), and a field gives events and counts, not rates. So the comparison is between the plan and a model fitted to the observations, not between the plan and raw data, and it must be quantitative: (ε, δ)-closeness and a behavioural distance, not yes or no. Repeated counts of the same plants on different dates are strongly dependent, which a fit must respect.

event log per plant or animal ──▶ fitted rates ──▶ fitted model │ plan (PEPA model) ──────────────────────────────┴─▶ compare: • same possible behaviour? (witness) • how far apart? (distance) • same long-run harvest rate?

Where to find it here. The Observations tab compares dated counts with the plan and fits rates; the Stream tab replays a plan and a bed observed event by event, with “holds so far” until a difference is final; the Compare tab checks two models exactly and says how far apart they are. Not yet connected in this tool: raw event logs as an input, and a long-run throughput comparison.

A caution about causes. This comparison is association, not causation. “Plot B is behind plan” can be read straight off the verdict; “the guild is working” cannot, because soil, weather and management differ between plots. Causal claims need a design behind them (a control plot, matched units) or a model whose structure you trust. Fitted rates from a handful of dated counts rarely tell competing mechanisms apart.

How far apart? Distances and tolerances

Measured rates are never exactly equal, so two real systems are almost never exactly the same. A yes/no answer is then too blunt. The Compare tab adds two softer answers under “How far apart?”.

  • A distance between 0 and 1. Run both models side by side in the way that makes a chosen count (say, healthy plants) look as alike as possible, and average how far apart the count still is, with the far future counting less (the half-life). 0 means they cannot be told apart through that count. The table lists the situations that add most to the distance.
  • A tolerance. Are the chances of each next step within ε of each other, and the speeds within a factor eδ? If so, the tool guarantees how much any outcome can differ by a given time. The guarantee is safe but often loose.

Both are estimates of how much two systems differ; neither replaces the exact verdict, which the Compare tab shows above them.

What-if, attribution and causes (§12)

Next to bisimulation, “causality” can mean two different things, and both are available. Neither is a claim about causes on the farm: they describe how a model behaves. The first changes rates and asks what follows; the second compares which steps depend on which.

Available (API) — what-if and attribution on the model. A PEPA model is a mechanism: setting a rate and re-deriving the chain asks “what does the model do if this rate were different?”. Two operations use it.

what-if set rates, compare the expected count of a state with the baseline attribute plan and fitted model differ in several rates, and so does the outcome: how much of the gap does each rate account for?

Attribution is a Shapley decomposition. Each differing rate is switched from its plan value to its fitted value, in every possible order; a rate’s contribution is its average effect on the outcome over those orders. By Shapley’s theorem the contributions add up to the gap exactly, whatever the interactions, and a rate the outcome does not depend on gets exactly zero. This is a convention, not the only reasonable split: it treats every order of switching as equally likely and starts from the plan, so for interacting rates a different split (one rate alone, or a variance-based one) gives other numbers; the tool reports the effect of each rate alone beside the Shapley value. The two models must differ only in the values of their parameters, which is checked, not assumed. At most 8 rates are decomposed (the cost is 2n evaluations). JSON operations whatif and attribute; the example attribute_tomatoes fits the tomato bed to its counts and shows ripening as the rate that explains most of the shortfall.

What this does and does not show. It says which rate the model has to change to reproduce the counts. That carries over to the farm only if the model’s structure is right, and a few dated counts rarely distinguish competing mechanisms. Evidence about what actually holds a plot back needs a design: a control plot or matched units left untreated. This tab has no button for these operations yet.

Available (API) — causality between events (history-preserving bisimulation). Ordinary bisimulation (§1) treats a run as a sequence of actions. History-preserving bisimulation also tracks which step enabled which. Each component of a PEPA model becomes a token moving through its local states (a safe Petri net), and a step depends on an earlier one when it moves a component that the earlier step moved. Two systems can perform the same actions at the same rates and still differ here. Two workers who water and weed independently give the same Markov chain as one farmer who does both, but for the farmer every task waits on the previous one. Likewise, with one vet each healing depends on the previous healing, which a second vet removes. The answer comes with a witness such as ⟨water ← ∅⟩⟨weed ← ∅⟩tt: “a watering that depends on nothing earlier, then a weeding that depends on nothing earlier either” holds for the two workers, not for the farmer. In ⟨a ← 1,2⟩, the numbers are the earlier steps the a depends on.

The method is decidable for finite safe nets (Vogler, ICALP 1991), and DEXPTIME-complete there (Jategaonkar and Meyer, TCS 1996). The finite form of the game used here, which groups past steps by the tokens that depend on them, is this library’s own construction. It is proved in Lean to decide the same thing as the definition, and the code is tested against the definition on random nets. Rates are left out. A model with functional rates (#Name) is refused, because such a rate can switch a step off, which the net cannot express. I found no source that combines causal bisimulation with rates, so a stochastic version would be new work. JSON operation causal; example causal_farm.

For the curious I: the formal story

The precise mathematics behind steps 2–7, at graduate level. Notation: ⟨a⟩φ “can do a, then φ”; ⟨a⟩=r φ “does a at total rate r into states where φ holds”; tt true.

§1. Behaviour as coalgebra; bisimulation as greatest fixpoint

A transition system with weights W (here: positive reals, or booleans if rates are ignored) is a coalgebra S: X → Pfin(A × W × X) for the finite-powerset-with-weights functor: a state is described by observing one layer of behaviour. Algebra folds structure into a value; coalgebra unfolds behaviour out of a state — the dual, and the right shape for “what can this system do next?”.

Bisimulation is the coinductive notion of sameness: a relation R ⊆ X × X such that matched states take R-matching steps with equal weights. Let F be the functional

F(R) = { (s,t) | for every a: the a-steps of s and t pair up into R-matching steps with equal weight }.

F is monotone on the complete lattice of binary relations, so it has a greatest fixpoint by Knaster–Tarski; bisimilarity ~ = νF is the largest symmetric fixpoint. This gives both proof principles:

Coinduction (to prove s ~ t): exhibit any R with (s,t) ∈ F(R). Induction (to refute s ~ t): exhibit a modal formula true of s only (§2).

§2. Modal logic and the Hennessy–Milner theorem

Hennessy–Milner logic observes one step at a time: φ ::= tt | ¬φ | φ ∧ φ | ⟨a⟩φ | ⟨a⟩=r φ. Its semantics is a monotone predicate lifting of S: s ⊨ ⟨a⟩φ iff some a-step lands in a state satisfying φ.

Theorem (Hennessy–Milner, image-finite case). For systems where each state has finitely many steps: s ~ t iff s and t satisfy exactly the same formulas. Depth-k formulas characterise ~k, the k-step approximation of bisimilarity.

Every failed comparison in this tool returns such a formula — a distinguishing witness, extracted from the refinement history (§4). Its nesting depth is the depth of the modal formula, i.e. how many steps deep the models must look before they differ.

§3. Partition refinement

Signature refinement computes ~ from above: start with the indiscrete partition P0 = {X}, and split blocks until stable.

signature_P(s)(a)(B) = Σ { w | s —a,w→ s′ ∈ S, s′ ∈ B } P′ = { states with equal signatures under P }.

~k is exactly the partition produced after k rounds, bisimilarity is the fixpoint, and on a finite system the sequence stabilises (each strict round at least doubles the block count of some state). The fast backend here is worklist-based (CoPaR-style): only predecessors of split blocks recompute signatures, cached per state, and the largest part of a split keeps its identity — Hopcroft’s bound, giving O(m log n) on m transitions and n states in practice.

§4. Distinguishing formulas as witnesses

When the refinement history separates s from t at round k, the block labels around the split assemble into a formula of depth k true of s, false of t: each round contributes one modal operator per separating action, with rate conditions ⟨a⟩=r when the split came from unequal weights. The formula is a certificate: you can check it by hand against the two transition lists, independent of the algorithm.

§5. Simulation: the one-sided preorder

Simulation drops the “match back” clause: s ≼ t when t can mimic every step of s (into states simulating the targets). It is again νF for a monotone functional, but on arbitrary — not necessarily symmetric — relations, so it is a preorder, and the induced equivalence is mutual simulation. Simulation is a closure operator (reflexive, monotone, idempotent); its modal characterisation uses only the positive fragment (no negation): s ≼ t iff t satisfies every formula s does, for negation-free φ. Note mutual simulation ⊋ bisimilarity can hold strictly, which is why this tool reports both.

§6. The bisimulation game

Equivalently, bisimilarity has a game characterisation (Stirling): Spoiler and Duplicator move tokens on the two models; from (s,t), Spoiler picks a step on either side, Duplicator must answer with a matching step; a matching pair moves the tokens to the targets. Duplicator wins infinite plays (the coinductive clause), Spoiler wins by reaching unmatched positions.

Theorem. s ~ t iff Duplicator has a winning strategy from (s,t).

The game solver in this repository explores pairs on demand from the initial pair — it can refute bisimilarity by finding a shallow Spoiler win without refining the whole product system. Budgets turn the algorithm into a sound three-way answer: proven within budget (Holds), refuted (Violated, with a modal witness), or Unknown/HoldsUpTo when the budget stops the search — never an unsound verdict.

For the curious II: chance, PEPA and limits

The precise mathematics behind steps 8–12.

§7. From branching to probabilities: lumping, uniformisation

Forget action labels, sum weights per state pair: a rate-labelled system yields a CTMC with generator Q (qst = total rate s→t, qss = −exit rate). The tool’s “lumped” view is the quotient by the coarsest ordinary lumpable partition: for all s,s′ in a block B and t,t′ in a block C, the rates into C agree. Ordinary lumpability is precisely the condition that the quotient chain is again Markovian — and, as the tests assert, it coincides with Markovian bisimulation, the weight-sensitive refinement of §3.

Uniformisation (Jensen). For Λ ≥ maxs exit(s), P = I + Q/Λ is stochastic (self-loops allowed) and p(t) = Σk e−Λt(Λt)k/k! · p0Pk. Same generator, same marginals; the series is truncated at machine precision.

Steady state is the left kernel πQ = 0 — computed by power iteration on P, which preserves stationary distributions because uniformisation is a Poisson embedding.

§8. PEPA: SOS for stochastic process algebras

PEPA (Hillston) builds large systems compositionally; structural operational semantics derives the coalgebra from the term. The cooperative law is the analytic core. With apparent rate raP(a) = sum of a-rates offered by P, two active offers (a,r1).P′, (a,r2).Q′ under ⟨a⟩-cooperation run at

rate = (r₁ / ra_P) · (r₂ / ra_Q) · min(ra_P, ra_Q).

The fractions split the joint activity between the partners; the slower side sets the pace (a rendezvous, not a product). A passive partner (rate ⊤, the algebraic top) is no bottleneck: the law reduces to the active side’s rate. This is why the vet in the herd example does not throttle recovery. Functional rates (a, f(#P)).P′ evaluate f in the global state; for f linear in population counts the derived rates are exactly Gillespie’s mass-action propensities, so the same model yields a Markov chain and an ODE with no manual translation. The algebra refuses mixed active/passive offers in one array — its apparent rate would be undefined — which is precisely the situation functional rates replace.

§9. Fluid limits

For populations of size n, the law of large numbers for CTMCs (Kurtz) says the occupancy process Xn(t)/n converges on finite horizons to the deterministic solution of ẋ = drift(x), where each move contributes rate(x) × stoichiometry. The fluid engine reuses the SOS derivation in floats to assemble this drift and integrates with RK4 (the tests verify 4th-order convergence: halving the step cuts the error sixteenfold). Fluid answers scale where enumeration cannot, and conservation of totals falls out of the stoichiometry.

§10. Approximate bisimulation

Measured rates are never exactly equal, and exact refinement shatters under noise. (ε, δ)-bisimulation for CTMCs (Spork, Baier, Katoen, Klüppelholz, Piribauer, CAV 2025) relaxes exact equality with an additive tolerance ε on transition probabilities and a multiplicative one δ on exit rates, and the authors bound time- and reward-bounded reachability for states related that way. This tool’s check is in that spirit but is not that definition: class-sum rates match within ε (absolute, in rate units) and exit rates within δ (relative), and the paper’s reachability bounds are not proved for it. The checker reports an approximate verdict with a behavioural distance instead of forcing equality — a quasi-lumpability check on a pair of states, with tolerance grouping documented as non-transitive (a perturbation bound, not a congruence).

Exercise 1 — Refine the cycle product by hand

Two independent copies of a three-step cycle (interleaving ‖, each copy autonomous). Write the global states as phase pairs, list each state’s outgoing signature (which actions, how many), and predict the fixpoint partition before looking.

Then: open State space, and toggle Full → Lump.

Answer

9 states, 18 transitions, 6 blocks after lumping. Round 1 already separates states by which multiset of actions they offer; the pairs {(1,2),(2,1)}, {(1,3),(3,1)}, {(2,3),(3,2)} survive to the fixpoint, while the three diagonal states (1,1),(2,2),(3,3) stay distinct — ⟨start⟩tt holds only on (1,1). Exercise 2 — Cooperation arithmetic and steady state

Two clients share a server on req: each client offers req at rate 2, the server at rate 3, plus each client done at 1 after waiting. Using the cooperation law, compute the total req-rate when 2 clients are ready, and when 1 is. Then solve πQ = 0 for the three states by hand.

Check the edge labels in State space and the steady-state column in the table (click a state to highlight it).

Answer

Two ready clients: apparent rates raC = 4, raS = 3; each client-server pair runs at (2/4)(3/3)·min(4,3) = 1.5, so the total edge req rate is 3.0 — the server is the bottleneck. One ready client: raC = 2, rate (2/2)(3/3)·min(2,3) = 2.0 — now the client side is. The balance equations 3π₀ = π₁, 2π₁ = 2π₂ give π = (1/7, 3/7, 3/7) ≈ (0.143, 0.429, 0.429), matching the tool to six digits.

Exercise 3 — A passive partner is never the bottleneck

In the herd model the vet waits passively (infty) on healing. Predict the total healing-, disease- and death-rates in the states with 1, 2 and 3 sick animals, from the cooperation law and independence of the healthy animals.

Click through the states in State space and read the edge rates.

Answer

Healing: k · 0.1 = 0.10, 0.20, 0.30 — with a passive partner the law reduces to the active side’s own rate, so the single vet can serve any number of animals without throttling. Disease: k · 0.02 for k healthy (0.06 at the initial state); death: k · 0.01. Every total is a sum of independent exponential clocks — the state-independent rate structure is exactly what makes the quotient Markovian here… except that the vet-free variant of Exercise 4 breaks it.

Exercise 4 — Rates ignored or rates that matter

Compare the server model with a variant whose service rate is 6 instead of 3. Which of the four verdicts should hold, and why? Predict the distinguishing formula for the failed one.

In Compare, read the verdicts; click the failing one to see the formula highlighted on both graphs.

Answer

Branching is identical (same actions, same structure), so bisimulation, mutual simulation and simulation all hold; only Markovian bisimulation fails, because the initial states do req at different total rates. Witness: ⟨req⟩=3 tt — true of the rate-3 model, false of the rate-6 model. This is the practical dividing line: use the rate-blind verdicts to compare designs, the rate-sensitive one to compare parameters.

Exercise 5 — Reading a distinguishing formula

Herd health versus the same model with the vet removed. One of the formulas below is produced by the tool; derive it yourself first: what can A’s initial state do in two steps that B’s cannot?

Open Compare and select the failing verdict; the states satisfying the formula are highlighted.

Answer

⟨disease⟩⟨healing⟩tt: in A, disease then healing is possible (the vet makes healing possible at all); in B, no state reachable in two steps offers healing. The rate-sensitive analogue is ⟨disease⟩=0.06 ⟨healing⟩=0.1 tt. Note the depth is 2 = the refinement round at which the initial states’ blocks separate — the number in the formula is literally the history of §3.

Exercise 6 — An algebraic law, verified by four algorithms

Claim: P1 ‖ P1 and P1[2] denote the same behaviour — interleaving sugar versus array syntax. State what each of the four verdicts would need to show, then check.

All four verdicts hold: the models are Markovian bisimilar. Exercise 7 — Symmetry reduction and where it stops

Two identical two-state components versus two whose a-rates differ (1 and 2). Predict the number of bisimulation blocks in each case, and name the exact hypothesis of ordinary lumpability that fails in the second.

Check Full versus Lump state counts (the full graph even lists the deadlock in duplicate — one per copy — which lumping identifies).

Answer

Identical: 16 states → 3 blocks {both cycling, one dead, both dead}. Mismatched rates: 16 → 6 — the cross-pairs stop matching because rates into the same block disagree, exactly the row-sum condition of §7: states s, s′ in a block must send equal total rate into every block, and the two copies no longer do. Symmetry reduction is free redundancy removal within equal-parameter groups; parameter heterogeneity is what makes population models blow up.

Exercise 8 — Mass action from the syntax

The functional model S = (infect, 0.3 * #I).I with 3 susceptibles and 1 infected: compute the total infect rate at the initial state and at (S:2, I:2), and connect it to the mass-action term βSI of the SIR ODE.

Read the rates in State space; then open Over time (set the unit to seconds) for the exact transient.

Answer

Initial: each of the 3 S-copies offers 0.3 · #I = 0.3, total 0.9 = 0.3 · 3 · 1 = βSI. At (2,2): 0.3 · 2 · 2 = 1.2. The derived chain is the stochastic counterpart of Ṡ = −βSI — one model, two semantics (20 full states lump to 11 count-states, and the fluid limit of §9 is the same drift integrated deterministically).

Exercise 9 — Transient to steady state

In the herd model Dead is absorbing. Predict the long-run probability of death from the structure of the graph (no computation), then verify against both the transient curve and the steady-state column.

Set the horizon to 100 s; hover the chart at the far right, and compare with the steady column of the Dead state in the table.

Answer

From every non-dead state there is a path to Dead and no path out, so Dead is the unique closed class: P(dead) → 1 and the steady-state mass of every other state is 0. Both the uniformisation series at t = 100 and π agree to displayed precision. (The vet delays the day of death; it does not change the limit — compare with Exercise 5’s transient formulas.)
